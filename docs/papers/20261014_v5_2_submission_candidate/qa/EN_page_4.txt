C. Class-Conditional Disagreement and Reference Support
The next question is whether this overall improvement
also preserves decisions across reference classes. At 30%
ofﬂine retention, Full/U reduced overall disagreement relative
to Boundary across all six consumers, but class-balanced
outcomes varied (Fig. 3).
SHOW instruction revealed a clear trade-off. Among the
same 79 participants with complete reference-class support,
Full/U reduced overall disagreement by a median of 2.608
percentage points (95% descriptive bootstrap interval: 1.708–
2.960), while class-balanced disagreement increased by 20.880
points (12.280–27.156). Thus, an aggregate improvement co-
incided with substantially worse class-balanced performance
within the same participants. In contrast, CeTI grasp improved
both overall and class-balanced disagreement by 4.261 and
18.31 points, respectively, with all 80 participants evaluable.
Selection also reduced the number of participants for whom
class-balanced disagreement could be evaluated. For DB9
posture, complete reference-class support was available in
22 of 80 participants without rejection, but only three under
Full/U at 30% retention. Its class-balanced comparison there-
fore describes only three jointly evaluable participants, not the
full cohort. Together, these ﬁndings show why overall dis-
agreement, class-balanced disagreement, and reference-class
support must be interpreted jointly.
IV. DISCUSSION
A shared pose interface should be evaluated not only by
whether a downstream model can accept its inputs, but also by
whether the model preserves its decisions. Across six frozen
consumers, learned ofﬂine ranking reduced overall disagree-
ment, yet its class-level consequences were not uniformly
beneﬁcial.
The distinction arises because overall disagreement weights
reference classes according to their frequency among retained,
reference-valid blocks, whereas class-balanced disagreement
gives each class equal weight. Improving one measure there-
fore does not guarantee improving the other. SHOW instruc-
tion illustrates this divergence within the same 79 evaluable
participants, although the experiment does not establish the
speciﬁc mechanism behind it. DB9 posture reveals a different
limitation: after selection, only three participants retained suf-
ﬁcient support for evaluating every reference class. These are
distinct outcomes—worsened class-balanced disagreement and
reduced evaluability—that overall disagreement alone would
not reveal.
Our conclusions concern the speciﬁed frozen estimator–
consumer interfaces. A ﬂip compares two model-generated
decisions rather than independently labeled action correctness.
Although pose mapping, source calibration procedures, and
temporal indexing were examined, cross-source absolute pose
equivalence and independent residual synchronization were
not established. The upstream checkpoint’s actual training
membership also remains unresolved. Finally, whole-record
processing and participant-speciﬁc retrospective ranking do
not demonstrate a deployable online rejection system.
V. CONCLUSION
Replacing measured hand pose with EMG-estimated pose
changed the decisions of six frozen pose consumers. Learned
ofﬂine ranking reduced overall disagreement relative to
boundary-based selection, but these gains did not guaran-
tee class-balanced improvement or sufﬁcient reference-class
support. The ﬁndings show that downstream decision ﬁdelity
should be assessed jointly through overall disagreement, class-
balanced disagreement, and evaluable class support. This is
evidence about speciﬁed frozen interfaces under retrospective
evaluation, not a claim of target-task correctness or online
deployment reliability.
